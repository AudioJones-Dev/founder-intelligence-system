# Adaptive Tension Tolerance

## Core Idea
Most people discharge tension as fast as it appears. They fill silences, soften asks, accept the first counter, or change the subject. The capacity to *sit inside* tension — without compulsively resolving it — is a structural advantage.

The brain treats interpersonal friction with roughly the same circuitry it uses for physical threat. Training the capacity to remain composed inside that circuitry is what allows you to hold a fair position when the other side expects you to flinch.

## When To Use It
- Negotiating price, terms, or scope
- Giving feedback that the receiver doesn't want to hear
- Refusing a request from someone senior to you
- Sitting with a decision long enough to make the right one rather than the comfortable one
- Holding a strategic line when an investor, board member, or customer pushes for the easy answer

## The Move
State the position. Specifically. Calmly. Then stop talking.

Treat the silence that follows as the most useful part of the conversation. The counterparty is now doing the work — either by surfacing new information, updating their position, or revealing what they actually need.

## What This Is Not
- Stubbornness — refusing to update when given new information is not tension tolerance, it's brittleness
- Aggression — raising the temperature isn't the same as tolerating it
- Stonewalling — silence as a weapon corrodes the relationship; silence as space invites movement

The test: when the conversation ends, is the other side more informed and more respected, or are they more cornered?

## Anti-Patterns
- Holding tension to win rather than to find the right answer
- Performing composure while leaking distress non-verbally
- Treating the tension itself as the goal — some conversations should resolve quickly
